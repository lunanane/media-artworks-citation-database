# The Laughing Snake

**Morehshin Allahyari**, 2018 · Online / net art / Text / print / Material / sculpture

The Laughing Snake is a hypertext narrative on the Whitney Museum's artport, co-commissioned with Liverpool Biennial and FACT for Liverpool Biennial 2018, and part of Allahyari's project She Who Sees The Unknown. It retells a myth from the Kitab al-bulhan (Book of Wonders) about a female snake who terrorises a city and dies laughing when shown her reflection in a mirror; after a narrated introduction the reader clicks hyperlinked passages, some leading to the artist's personal stories of growing up in Iran. An installation version with a 3D-printed sculpture, mirrored panels and a touch screen is in the Whitney collection.

## In their words

> I wanted to turn this story around; to embrace the monstrosity; to change this image and the structure. Through writing a new story about The Laughing Snake, I chose to show that she is not laughing at her own reflection, but rather at her reflection within the world she lives in.
>
> — Morehshin Allahyari, [Joel Kuennen, 'Refiguring Monstrosity: Morehshin Allahyari // Whitney Museum of American Art', The Seen, 2018](https://theseenjournal.org/?p=14976)

<sub>says</sub>

> Her laughing, and subsequent death, is a powerful gesture to take her reflection back—I use that to tell real personal stories about the past and present of my life and an imagined future.
>
> — Morehshin Allahyari, [Joel Kuennen, 'Refiguring Monstrosity', The Seen, 2018](https://theseenjournal.org/?p=14976)

<sub>says</sub>

> The story of “The Laughing Snake” is about the female image and reflection. It is about death and laughter. It is about winning and losing. I wanted to take all these concepts and relate them to my personal experiences.
>
> — Morehshin Allahyari, [Joel Kuennen, 'Refiguring Monstrosity', The Seen, 2018](https://theseenjournal.org/?p=14976)

<sub>says</sub>

> It is not so much about wanting to keep old myths alive, but instead about wanting to create worlds around these figures who are forgotten and misrepresented through 1500+ years of history.
>
> — Morehshin Allahyari, [Joel Kuennen, 'Refiguring Monstrosity', The Seen, 2018](https://theseenjournal.org/?p=14976)

<sub>says</sub>

> To remind women, femmes, and the people of the Middle East that the figures within our stories (both fictional and actual) matter—not just for the present, but for claiming an alternative future.
>
> — Morehshin Allahyari, [Joel Kuennen, 'Refiguring Monstrosity', The Seen, 2018](https://theseenjournal.org/?p=14976)

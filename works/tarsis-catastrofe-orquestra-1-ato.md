# catástrofe orquestra #1 (Ato I)

**Antonio Társis**, 2025 · Sound / Material / sculpture / Robotics / kinetics

An installation made for the 36th Bienal de São Paulo (2025): 11 suspended panels of recycled matchbox fragments on thin wooden slats, among which 64 motor-driven rope pendulums carrying pieces of coal strike leather drums. The coal shatters on impact; the sounds are picked up by microphones and amplified into a soundscape with noises and voices, and the debris accumulates on the floor. The work later travelled with the Bienal's itinerant exhibitions, including to the Pinacoteca do Ceará and the Centro Cultural La Moneda in Santiago.

## In their words

> That is what a catastrophe is: everything comes crashing down. A catastrophe is never the final silence, but the exact moment when matter under stress is forced to emit its strongest and deepest frequency. My questions have been heading in exactly that direction: how can we orchestrate a great ruin? How can we understand the way in which we are able to compose the very collapse of the world, of life, and of death?
>
> — Antonio Társis, ["Catastrophe orchestra: an interview with Antonio Társis", Fundação Bienal de São Paulo](https://bienal.org.br/en/catastrophe-orchestra-an-interview-with-antonio-tarsis/)

<sub>asks</sub>

> The history of humanity is like a great battle of materialities. When I received the invitation from the Bienal, it was a great opportunity to reflect on the materials I’ve been researching and how they might collide with one another, generating friction. The matchboxes that carry fire. Coal, which is essentially a fossilized, burned tree; which fueled industrial production in the past century; which brought us to our current technological model—driven and shaped by the historical rhythms that fire (and water) have created over time. In catástrofe orquestra #1 (Ato I), I am interested in the violent act of the coal pendulums being dropped onto the drum as a representation of this historical thread.
>
> — Antonio Társis, ["Catastrophe orchestra: an interview with Antonio Társis", Fundação Bienal de São Paulo](https://bienal.org.br/en/catastrophe-orchestra-an-interview-with-antonio-tarsis/)

<sub>says</sub>

> I sought to compose these relationships with materials and historical events: extraction, which transforms matter into technology; technology, which reorganizes behaviors, emotions, ways of life, and violence. One thing overlapping another, like a massive fire having a domino effect or a large orchestra in which each instrument has its own function and note. The catastrophe ceases to be an isolated event and comes to be seen as a functioning system, governing our lives at every moment.
>
> — Antonio Társis, ["Catastrophe orchestra: an interview with Antonio Társis", Fundação Bienal de São Paulo](https://bienal.org.br/en/catastrophe-orchestra-an-interview-with-antonio-tarsis/)

<sub>says</sub>

> I think my work isn’t exactly about objects and their packaging, it’s about the abundance of hidden history within things considered unimportant, or that are so present in our daily lives that we don’t even notice them.
>
> — Antonio Társis, ["Catastrophe orchestra: an interview with Antonio Társis", Fundação Bienal de São Paulo](https://bienal.org.br/en/catastrophe-orchestra-an-interview-with-antonio-tarsis/)
